> **Opening question:** If a dashboard shows a value at your location, what do you need to know before using that value in a decision or public conversation?

## At a glance

In this lesson, you will:

- Identify what a public map or dashboard claims to display.
- Read its legend, indicators, geography, time period, and source.
- Test its interactive controls deliberately.
- State one interpretation the map supports and one it does not.

**Time:** 35 minutes  
**You will produce:** A plain-language explanation of a public dashboard and a short limitations statement.

## Why this matters

Public dashboards can make complex data appear immediate: search an address, select a date, and receive a color, score, or measurement. The interface may feel self-explanatory even when the underlying data is not. A useful public map must therefore be read at three levels:

1. **Interface:** what can the user click, select, or view?
2. **Data:** what was measured, estimated, categorized, or reported?
3. **Claim:** what interpretation does the map support?

This distinction is especially important when maps inform community meetings, environmental decisions, news reports, or student research.

## Step 1: State the dashboard's purpose

Open the map without clicking anything. Locate its title, introductory text, and sponsoring organization.

Complete this sentence:

> This dashboard is designed to help its users understand or do...

Then ask whether the map is primarily intended to:

- Monitor current conditions
- Show historical patterns
- Compare places
- Communicate modeled risk
- Report incidents or complaints
- Locate services or infrastructure
- Support research or policy decisions

A dashboard can serve more than one purpose, but identifying its central purpose makes later interpretation more disciplined.

## Step 2: Identify the indicator

Find the map's legend, information panel, tooltip, or metadata link. Write down:

- The indicator or variable name
- Its unit of measurement
- Whether it is a count, rate, percentage, category, index, model result, or sensor measurement
- The time period it represents
- The geographic unit or location represented

Do not translate the indicator into a conclusion yet. First establish what the value literally represents.

For example, a sensor reading at one location is not automatically a measure of conditions throughout a neighborhood. A complaint count is not identical to the number of events that occurred; it is also shaped by awareness, access, reporting behavior, and administrative practices.

## Step 3: Read the geography

Ask what each mapped object represents:

- A sensor location?
- An address?
- A census tract?
- A ZIP code?
- A watershed?
- A political or administrative area?
- A modeled surface?

Zooming in may create the impression of greater precision without changing the resolution of the underlying data. A colored polygon remains an aggregate area even when the street beneath it becomes visible.

Record whether the map shows an exact location, a summarized area, or an estimate between observations.

## Step 4: Test one control at a time

Interactive maps may include date selectors, category filters, comparison tools, layer controls, animation, and pop-ups. Change only one control at a time and record what changes.

Use this sequence:

1. Select one place.
2. Record the current time or date setting.
3. Change one filter.
4. Observe whether the legend, scale, symbols, or visible records change.
5. Return to the original setting before testing another control.

This avoids comparing two views that differ in several hidden ways.

## Step 5: Locate the source and update schedule

Find the data source, collection method, and last-updated date. If this information is not visible, note that absence.

Ask:

- Who collects the data?
- How is it collected: sensor, survey, administrative record, model, crowdsourcing, or another method?
- How frequently is it updated?
- Does the map show real-time information, delayed reporting, or a historical archive?
- Are missing values explained?

The phrase “real time” deserves particular attention. It may describe frequent updates to an interface without guaranteeing that every underlying record is current or complete.

## Step 6: Write one supported interpretation

Use this structure:

> For **[place or geographic unit]**, the dashboard shows **[indicator]** at **[value or category]** during **[time period]**, based on data collected by **[source or method]**.

Then add one cautious interpretation:

> This suggests...

Avoid causal language unless the data and documentation support it. A spatial pattern can identify a difference or association without explaining why it exists.

## Critical pause

Ask what conditions affect the dashboard's public meaning:

- Who can use the interface comfortably?
- Does it work on a phone or only on a large screen?
- Can the information be printed or explained without the interactive interface?
- Are the terms understandable without specialized knowledge?
- Which communities are well represented by the data collection system?
- How can residents question or supplement the mapped information?

In a community meeting, the map should be treated as one source of knowledge. Participant experience may reveal flooding, access barriers, maintenance problems, or neighborhood conditions that the dashboard was not designed to record.

## Step 7: State one limitation

Complete one or more statements:

- This dashboard cannot tell us...
- This value applies to..., not necessarily to...
- The map may underrepresent...
- A user could misread the map by assuming...
- We would need additional information about...

## Checkpoint

- [ ] I can name the indicator, unit, geographic unit, and time period.
- [ ] I can identify the data source and update schedule—or state that they are missing.
- [ ] I can explain what changes when I use a filter.
- [ ] I can write one supported interpretation and one limitation.

## Key takeaway

Reading a dashboard means more than locating a value. A responsible interpretation connects the interface to the underlying data and connects the data to a limited, clearly stated claim.
