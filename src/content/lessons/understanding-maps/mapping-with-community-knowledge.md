> **Opening question:** At which decisions does a mapping project become genuinely community-led?

## At a glance

In this lesson, you will:

- Distinguish participation from shared decision-making.
- Identify the forms of knowledge a community may bring to a map.
- Plan who will shape the question, categories, interpretation, and use.
- Establish initial agreements about consent, ownership, access, and maintenance.

**Time:** 40 minutes  
**You will produce:** A one-page participatory mapping process plan.

## Why this matters

A map can be made *about* a community, *for* a community, *with* a community, or *by* a community. Those phrases can describe very different relationships.

In a conventional project, a researcher or organization may define the question, select the data, choose the categories, build the map, and then invite residents to comment on the finished result. The meeting may be participatory, but most of the decisions have already been made.

Community-led mapping begins earlier. Participants help decide what the project should ask, what counts as evidence, which places and experiences matter, how sensitive information will be handled, and what should happen after the map is made. Technical expertise still matters, but it supports a shared process rather than silently controlling it.

## Step 1: Name the purpose before choosing the tool

Imagine that residents want to document recurring street flooding that is not fully visible in an official dashboard.

Several mapping purposes are possible:

- Help neighbors understand where flooding has been observed.
- Support a request for drainage improvements.
- Compare community observations with sensor or service-request data.
- Record routes that become difficult or unsafe during storms.
- Preserve residents' accounts of how flooding has changed.

These purposes are related, but they are not interchangeable. A map intended for internal organizing may contain information that should not appear on a public website. A map intended to influence an agency may need documentation that a storytelling map does not. Before collecting locations, complete this sentence:

> We want to make a map so that **[audience]** can **[use or decision]**.

Then ask: Who defined that purpose? Who should be able to revise it?

## Step 2: Recognize different forms of community knowledge

Community knowledge is not simply a less formal version of institutional data. It may reveal different conditions, boundaries, histories, and priorities.

Participants may contribute:

- Observations from daily routines and repeated experiences
- Local names for places or boundaries that do not appear in official files
- Memories of change over time
- Photographs, sketches, oral histories, or walking routes
- Knowledge of access, danger, care, culture, or belonging
- Explanations of why an official category does not fit local experience
- Questions that an existing dataset was never designed to answer

Deciding what counts as data is therefore part of the mapping process. A pin on a map may be useful, but it cannot automatically represent the meaning of a place, the duration of an event, or disagreement among participants.

### Pause and notice

For your proposed project, list three forms of knowledge that an official dataset may not contain. Do not immediately convert them into columns. First ask what might be lost in that conversion.

## Step 3: Locate participation in the project

Use the following table to examine where decisions currently sit:

| Project decision | Current decision-maker | Who is affected? | How could authority be shared? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Define the question | | | |
| Decide who participates | | | |
| Decide what counts as evidence | | | |
| Choose categories and names | | | |
| Select the mapping method | | | |
| Validate observations | | | |
| Interpret patterns | | | |
| Decide what becomes public | | | |
| Correct or remove information | | | |
| Maintain the map | | | |

Participation is not one event. It can occur at every stage, and its depth can vary. Asking people to place dots on a predesigned map gives them a role in data collection. It does not necessarily give them authority over the question, categories, interpretation, or final use.

## Step 4: Choose methods that fit the participants

The most sophisticated platform is not necessarily the most appropriate method. Consider:

- **Paper mapping:** useful when internet access, devices, accounts, or digital confidence vary.
- **Walking interviews:** useful when observations emerge through movement and conversation.
- **Story circles or oral histories:** useful when the meaning of place cannot be reduced to coordinates.
- **Sticker or annotation activities:** useful for group discussion around a shared base map.
- **Mobile or web forms:** useful for distributed collection when participants have suitable devices and connectivity.
- **GIS workshops:** useful when participants want to build technical capacity and retain control of the files.
- **Hybrid methods:** useful when paper, conversation, photographs, and digital records need to work together.

Evaluate the method against language, disability access, time, transportation, device access, data costs, privacy, and the emotional or political sensitivity of the topic. Provide more than one way to participate when possible.

## Step 5: Establish agreements before collecting information

Consent is not only a signature at the beginning. Participants need enough information to make choices throughout the project.

Discuss and record:

1. **Purpose:** What will the information be used for?
2. **Visibility:** Will the map be private, shared with a group, or public?
3. **Attribution:** Will contributors be named, anonymous, or represented collectively?
4. **Sensitivity:** Which locations or stories should be generalized, delayed, restricted, or omitted?
5. **Ownership:** Who holds the original files, account credentials, and exported copies?
6. **Review:** Who checks categories, locations, translations, and interpretations?
7. **Correction:** How can a participant change or remove a contribution?
8. **Future use:** Can the information be reused in research, teaching, advocacy, or another platform?
9. **Maintenance:** Who will update the map, and for how long?

Avoid promising anonymity if precise locations, timestamps, photographs, or narrative details could identify a contributor indirectly.

## Step 6: Plan validation as a collective interpretation

Validation does not mean forcing every contribution into agreement with an official dataset. It means checking what a record represents, whether it has been placed and described appropriately, and how differing accounts should be shown.

A community review session might ask:

- Does this location appear in the right place?
- Does the category describe what happened?
- Is the wording understandable and respectful?
- Are there patterns participants recognize?
- Are apparent gaps true absences, or places where participation was lower?
- Do participants disagree about the meaning or boundary of a place?
- Should disagreement remain visible rather than be resolved into one answer?

The resulting map may include notes about uncertainty, multiple boundaries, quotations, or layers representing different perspectives.

## Critical pause

Inviting participation does not automatically redistribute control. Ask:

- Who owns the platform account and can change the map?
- Who can download the underlying information?
- Who decides when the project is finished?
- Who receives credit, funding, or professional benefit?
- Can the community refuse publication after seeing the assembled map?
- What happens if an institution leaves but the community still needs the resource?

A project can use participatory methods while remaining institutionally controlled. Naming that tension is part of responsible planning.

## Step 7: Draft your process plan

Complete the following one-page plan:

### Participatory mapping process plan

**Purpose:**  
We want to make a map so that...

**Participants:**  
The people who should shape this project include...

**Knowledge and evidence:**  
The project will make room for...

**Participation methods:**  
People can contribute through...

**Shared decisions:**  
Participants will help decide...

**Consent and sensitivity:**  
We will protect choice and sensitive information by...

**Review and interpretation:**  
Before publication, the group will...

**Ownership and access:**  
The files and account access will be held by...

**Maintenance:**  
After the first map is produced...

**Open question:**  
We still need to decide...

## Checkpoint

- [ ] I can distinguish consultation from shared decision-making.
- [ ] I identified at least five decisions that participants should help govern.
- [ ] I included forms of knowledge beyond ready-made institutional data.
- [ ] I proposed more than one accessible way to participate.
- [ ] I addressed consent, sensitivity, ownership, review, and maintenance.
- [ ] I identified one unresolved question to bring to participants rather than answering it for them.

## Key takeaway

Community mapping is not defined by who supplies the points. It is defined by how authority moves through the project: who frames the question, defines the evidence, interprets the result, controls access, and decides what the map becomes.
