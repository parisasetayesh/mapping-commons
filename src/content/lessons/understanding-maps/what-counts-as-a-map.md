> **Opening question:** When you hear the word *map*, what do you picture—and what might that picture exclude?

## At a glance

In this lesson, you will:

- Compare conventional and less familiar forms of mapping.
- Examine how maps select, organize, and communicate relationships.
- Develop a working definition you can revise as you continue learning.

**Time:** 30 minutes  
**You will produce:** A short definition of a map and a close reading of one familiar example.

## Why this matters

Many people encounter mapping for the first time through software. The screen presents layers, coordinates, and buttons before the learner has decided what a map is supposed to do. Beginning with the concept changes the order: first identify the relationship or experience you want to represent; then decide whether a map—and which kind of map—can help.

## Step 1: Recall an everyday map

Think of a map you used, encountered, or created during the past week. It might have been:

- A subway or bus map
- A navigation app
- A weather or flood dashboard
- A campus diagram
- A seating chart
- A hand-drawn route
- A diagram of relationships
- A map in a news story

Write down what the map helped you understand or do.

### Pause and notice

- Did the map represent physical distance accurately?
- Did accuracy matter to its purpose?
- What information did it emphasize?
- What did it simplify or remove?

A subway map may distort distance and direction while making connections easier to follow. A hand-drawn route may omit most streets but preserve the landmarks needed by its intended reader. These are not necessarily failures. They are choices connected to purpose.

## Step 2: Look for organized relationships

A useful starting definition is:

> A map is a selective representation that organizes relationships so someone can interpret, navigate, compare, remember, or act.

Many maps organize geographic relationships: proximity, direction, boundary, movement, density, or change across space. Others map concepts, bodies, memories, networks, emotions, or institutions. What connects them is not a particular appearance. It is the work of selecting elements and arranging meaningful relationships among them.

Consider these examples:

1. A flood-risk map relates modeled water exposure to geographic areas.
2. A community asset map relates services, resources, and local knowledge to places.
3. A cognitive map records how a person remembers or experiences an environment.
4. A network map relates people or organizations through connections rather than physical distance.
5. A historical map may represent how a past institution understood territory—not simply where things were.

## Step 3: Separate the map from the territory

A map is not the place, community, event, or system it represents. It is a constructed account of selected aspects of that subject.

Every map therefore involves decisions:

- What is included?
- What is grouped together?
- What is named?
- What is treated as a boundary?
- What becomes a point, line, area, category, or connection?
- At what scale is the subject shown?
- What is left outside the frame?

Calling a map selective does not mean that it is false. It means that interpreting a map requires understanding the choices that made it possible.

## Step 4: Analyze one map

Return to your everyday example. Complete the following sentences:

1. **This map was made to help someone...**
2. **It organizes relationships among...**
3. **It emphasizes...**
4. **It simplifies or leaves out...**
5. **Its intended audience appears to be...**
6. **A different person or community might map this subject by...**

Your answers do not need to be technical. The goal is to notice that a map's purpose, structure, and audience are connected.

## Critical pause

The power to map often includes the power to name, classify, bound, and make claims appear stable. Ask:

- Who had the authority or resources to produce this map?
- Whose knowledge was treated as legitimate?
- Could the people represented change or challenge the map?
- Who benefits when this particular representation is used?

These questions apply to government maps, scientific models, commercial platforms, activist maps, and community projects. A participatory process may redistribute some mapping authority, but it still requires choices about facilitation, inclusion, technology, and ownership.

## Step 5: Write a working definition

Complete this statement in your own words:

> A map is...

Then add:

> A map is useful when...

> A map should be questioned when...

Keep this definition. Later lessons may complicate or revise it.

## Checkpoint

- [ ] I can recognize mapping beyond conventional geographic maps.
- [ ] I can identify relationships a map organizes.
- [ ] I can distinguish a mapped representation from the subject it represents.
- [ ] I can ask who produced a map, for whom, and for what purpose.

## Key takeaway

A map does not simply display a world that already exists in mapped form. It selects, organizes, and communicates relationships for a purpose. Learning to map therefore begins with questions of meaning, audience, and representation—not with software.
